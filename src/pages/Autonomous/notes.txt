The project focuses on a conversion kit that transforms regular boats into autonomous vessels, providing an accessible and cost-effective solution. The kit includes both mechanical hardware and software components to enable varying levels of autonomy. It features mechanisms for steering and throttle control, integrated with a central computer managing sensors, communications, and mechanical functions. Designed for compatibility, the kit can be installed on any boat meeting basic physical requirements.
The system offers scalable autonomy options, including cruise control, remote control, drive assist, collision warning, path following, and full autonomy. This modularity ensures flexibility for various use cases, enabling users to choose the autonomy level that fits their needs. The approach of converting existing boats instead of custom-built USVs reduces costs significantly, while the ability to retrofit a second-hand boat within hours eliminates long engineering and production timelines. Its mass-producible design allows rapid large-scale deployment, ideal for scenarios requiring fleets of USVs in tight timeframes.
The kit includes a suite of sensors such as GPS for navigation, IMU for motion sensing, radar for obstacle detection, cameras for visual input, and AIS for vessel tracking. These sensors work in tandem with the central computer, enabling precise and efficient task execution, whether guided by external commands or operating autonomously.
A key aspect of the product is the accompanying software environment. This platform provides real-time control of the primary USV, displaying sensor data for situational awareness.   It also supports fleet management by connecting and managing multiple USVs through a centralized system. As autonomy levels increase, the software scales to manage more units, offering seamless oversight and coordination for large-scale operations.
This conversion kit presents a transformative approach to autonomous vessel technology, offering cost-effective, scalable, and flexible solutions. By enabling rapid conversion of existing boats, it meets the growing demand for USVs across various sectors while reducing barriers to adoption. With its innovative hardware and software integration, the kit paves the way for broader deployment of USVs in diverse applications.
Preliminary Research and Project Scope

Leading up to the start of this project, extensive preliminary research was conducted into various technologies, markets, and possibilities surrounding maritime autonomy. Multiple scopes for the project were considered, including focusing on long-range sustainable propulsion for autonomous vessels, advanced meteorology systems, and constructing the physical ‘drone’ vessel itself using existing software.
Ultimately, the decision was made to prioritize the software and electrical hardware aspects of autonomy rather than the vessel's physical construction. Instead, a host vessel will be utilized to implement and test the autonomous features.

Research and Discovery Phase
The research and discovery phase encompassed three main disciplines:
Market Research
This phase examined existing solutions and players in the field of maritime autonomy. A detailed analysis was conducted to identify other companies and initiatives, as well as their specialized products. This research highlighted key industry figures, potential stakeholders, and collaborators. A significant focus was also placed on understanding the rules and regulations governing maritime autonomy.
Scientific Data and Requirements
Since the project involves engineering a system to interact with the physical world, gathering preliminary data was essential. This included determining controls such as torque and speed requirements, communication speeds, and sensor data. The insights gained from this phase served as the foundation for establishing technical requirements for the subsequent build.
User Research
With the project’s scope defined, identifying the user group became a key priority. Understanding who the product is designed to serve, their requirements, profiles, and intended use of the system helped shape the project's design goals. This phase further refined the system’s requirements and objectives.

Determining Components and Project Workflow
Once the requirements were clearly established, the next step was to identify and research the necessary components and stages of development. Questions such as which PLC (Programmable Logic Controller) is most suitable, which sensors are required, how components will be housed, and how communication systems will function were addressed. Additionally, interdependencies between processes were analyzed to ensure an efficient and logical project workflow. This comprehensive planning was integral to designing a cohesive and functional autonomous system.

Automating and digitizing a vessel introduces significant risks that must be carefully addressed, as they pertain to vessel control and global data exchange. Autonomous decision-making offers benefits, but without proper safeguards, it poses severe consequences. Below are the key risks, their implications, and mitigation strategies.

Data Security
Autonomous vessels rely on extensive data collection and transfer. Securing this data is critical to prevent unauthorized access or loss. Communication pipelines are protected through VPN tunnelling and encryption, while data is stored securely either locally or on online databases. Continuous updates to security protocols are essential to combat evolving cyber threats.

Control of the Vessel
Digital systems controlling vessels face vulnerabilities, such as the risk of misuse or hijacking. Mitigation strategies include VPN-protected communications, encryption, and user authentication protocols. For critical cases, ensuring the vessel operates offline can eliminate external interference. However, absolute prevention remains a challenge.

Collision and Damage Risks
Human operators can react instantaneously to unforeseen circumstances, whereas remote systems are constrained by programming and network latency. This increases the risk of collisions. Automated "dead man’s switches" halt the vessel under conditions like loss of connection, invalid sensor data, or lack of human intervention.

Validity of Data
Autonomous systems depend on accurate data, and corrupted data—from faulty sensors or external interference—can lead to serious consequences. Rigorous data validation filters ensure only reliable data is processed.

Additional Risks
Software Vulnerabilities: Bugs or flaws in the system could compromise safety. Regular audits and updates are vital. 
Environmental Factors: Extreme weather can disrupt operations. Redundant sensors and robust modeling help mitigate this. 
Interference and Jamming: Signal jamming could disrupt control. Anti-jamming measures and redundant communication channels are critical. 
Ethical and Legal Issues: Accountability in accidents needs clear frameworks. 

Final Thoughts
While these measures address critical risks, no system is foolproof. Continued research, collaboration, and testing are essential to advance autonomous vessel technology safely. Comprehensive risk assessments must evolve alongside the technology, ensuring responsible deployment.
The Unmanned Surface Vehicle (USV) sector is rapidly expanding, with increasing demand for autonomous maritime solutions in both military and civilian applications. USVs are classified into four categories based on length, each catering to different operational needs. This document focuses on the smaller categories, where complexity and cost are reduced compared to larger vessels.

Naval Applications
The initial focus of this project is on small and very small USVs, which are simpler in design and operation. The autonomous system developed excludes specific payload functionalities and vessel ratings, allowing flexibility for boat owners to customize their systems. Applications in this sector include:
IS&R (Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance): Relaying communication and gathering intelligence over extended missions. 
Kamikaze Missions: These require precise programming due to high stakes but have straightforward operational profiles. 
More complex applications, such as mine-sweeping, armed escorts, anti-submarine warfare (ASW), and anti-piracy, are outside the current scope due to their specialized requirements.

Commercial Applications
The USV package provides the foundational infrastructure to convert boats for autonomous operations, making it adaptable to various use cases. Initially, the focus is on the boat rental market, which offers a lower barrier to entry and significant potential for scaling. Key use cases include:
Recreational Boat Rentals: 
Simplified autonomy systems can assist inexperienced users by providing navigation support, auto-pilot functionality, and safety features. 
The system enables remote monitoring for rental companies, relaying real-time location and operational data. 
In the Netherlands, platforms like Botentehuur.nl and Click&Boat highlight a vibrant rental market, with thousands of recreational boats available for hire. An estimated 3000 vessels operate within this segment, presenting a promising target market for initial deployment.

Private Applications
While the private market for autonomous vessels remains nascent, it presents significant potential. Private recreational motorboats share operational profiles with rental boats, focusing on ease of use and safety features. Use cases include:

Autonomous features such as preprogrammed paths, lane-keeping, and obstacle avoidance enhance the user experience. 
The Netherlands alone has approximately 126,000 private recreational boats that meet the specifications for this project. These vessels, primarily under 7 meters in length with outboard engines, represent a substantial market segment for future exploration.

The development of autonomous vessels will progress through a phased roadmap aligned with increasing levels of autonomy. Each phase builds on the previous, integrating more complex features and capabilities.

Autonomy Levels
Level 0: Establishes the foundational framework for remote control, including a central computer, mechanical control components, and basic sensors (camera, GPS).
Level 1: Adds enhanced data inputs (e.g., inertial measurement unit) to provide navigation assistance, including predictive path visualization.
Level 2: Enables path-following autonomy using GPS and measurements. Operators still maintain oversight to handle external changes.
Level 3: Introduces obstacle avoidance and environmental adaptation. Additional sensors, like radar and cameras, improve situational awareness, although operators remain on standby.
Level 4: Achieves semi-autonomy with independent decision-making under certain conditions, such as docking and weather adjustments. Requires advanced sensors (e.g., Lidar) and machine learning for environment interaction.
Level 5: Marks full autonomy, allowing vessels to operate entirely independently, including route planning, obstacle management, and docking.

Conceptual Roadmap
Phase 0: Conceptualization (1 month, Aug–Sep 2024)
Focus on defining the product concept, writing the business plan, and outlining technical and functional requirements. Deliverables include business and financial plans.
Phase 1: Proof of Concept (4 months, Sep 2024–Jan 2025)
Build a prototype to demonstrate basic functionality. Establish a supply chain, engage stakeholders, and gather initial feedback. Key outputs: proof-of-concept and feedback summary.
Phase 2: MVP Development (7 months, Jan–Aug 2025)
Develop a Minimum Viable Product (MVP), refine business strategies, and explore funding opportunities. Deliverables include an MVP and a funding strategy.
Phase 3: Product Refinement (5 months, Sep 2025–Jan 2026)
Refine the MVP based on feedback, develop a go-to-market strategy, and secure funding. Outputs include an enhanced product version and pilot program.
Phase 4: Market Preparation (6 months, Feb–Aug 2026)
Scale production, establish supply chains, and onboard early customers in preparation for market launch.
This phased approach ensures structured progression toward achieving full autonomy while integrating market readiness and scalability.
